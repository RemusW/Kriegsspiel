Lesson 0006 · Kriegsspiel network model

Smoothing what arrives late

Updates arrive in lumps. The screen draws continuously. Everything in between is invented, and there are exactly two respectable ways to invent it. You have already built one of them, for a different reason, without calling it by its networking name — and your own notes have already ruled out the other. This lesson is mostly about recognising what you have.

00First, from memory

  1. A store is best described as which of these?

    • A cache of server state
    • A subscription list, not storage
    • The single source of truth
    • A place for shared values

    A subscription. The storage is free; the notification is the price, which is why the change rate of a value decides where it belongs.

  2. Why were per-unit deltas rejected?

    • Snapshots compress better over sockets
    • The client cannot apply them correctly
    • A delta needs every earlier one
    • Serialization costs would have risen

    A delta is only correct for a client holding every earlier one, and the watch channel's coalescing deliberately will not promise that. Hold on to this shape — it reappears below.

  3. What is on screen partway through a drag?

    • The board plus pending edits
    • The server's latest board alone
    • The engine's own copy of Doc
    • Whatever the springs have reached

    Board plus PendingEdits, which is what picking and every new edit measure against. The springs are a fourth thing again — what is drawn — and this lesson is about that gap.

01The gap nobody can avoid

Any networked display has the same hole in it. Authoritative state arrives at some discrete rate — for your boards, whenever a human does something; for presence, a planned twenty times a second. The screen asks what to draw sixty or a hundred and forty-four times a second. Between two updates, every frame is a guess.

Snapping to the latest value is the one answer that is always wrong. Gambetta puts the symptom plainly: you get “players who teleport short distances every 100 ms.” The discreteness of the network becomes visible in the motion, which is the one place it must never show up.

So you invent the in-between. Two strategies, and they differ in what they are willing to be wrong about.

02Strategy one: render the past

This is entity interpolation, and the trick is in the name of the trade-off rather than the maths. From Gambetta, Part III:

“The key to the solution is to show the other players in the past relative to the user’s player. […] This way you’re always showing the user actual movement data, except you’re showing it 100 ms ‘late’.”

The deciding property, stated on the same page: interpolation “uses only real entity data provided by the server.” It never guesses a position that was not sent.

You hold a buffer of timestamped samples and draw at now − delay, between the two samples that straddle it. The path on screen is exactly the path the server described. You pay for that with a fixed added latency, and with state: at minimum two timestamped samples per entity, and a clock you trust.

Its documented costs are worth reading as a checklist, because for you three of the four land harmlessly. “Every player sees a slightly different rendering of the game world” — fine, umpires are not competing. But: “the illusion breaks down when an event needs high spatial and temporal accuracy, such as shooting at a moving target,” because “you’re shooting where your target was 100 ms ago.” You have no such event. Nothing in Kriegsspiel resolves against a moving position.

03Strategy two: chase the latest

The other answer keeps no history at all. Hold one target, move towards it continuously, and when a new update arrives simply replace the target. A low-pass filter on a sequence of setpoints.

You wrote this months ago. PawnLayer.sync, once per board:

client/src/engine/pawns/PawnLayer.ts:150

view.motion.target = unit;

One assignment. No array, no timestamp, no buffer — the previous target is simply gone. Then step walks each spring towards it at a fixed 240 Hz, and update drains an accumulator so the behaviour is frame-rate independent.

The recognition

spring.ts is not “animation code that happens to sit near the network layer”. It is a complete implementation of the second smoothing strategy, and the only reason it does not look like networking code is that you built it to make blocks feel physical. The network benefit was free. A remote umpire's edit slides in rather than teleporting, and that was never the feature you were writing.

And your own doc comment already states the invariant that makes it safe: the units’ positions “are the targets, so a block may trail its unit for a moment but never changes where the unit is.”

That sentence is the drawing/truth separation, written down before you had a name for why it mattered. Picking and editing read the unit; only the eye reads the spring.

04Your notes already chose, and did not say so

Here is the finding. The presence section of CLAUDE.md lists four rules. One of them is:

“Throttle updates (a starting guess is around 20 a second), let the receiving engine smooth between them, and drop outdated ones instead of queueing them.”

CLAUDE.md, open questions, presence.

Read that against strategy one. Entity interpolation is a queue — it cannot function without retaining at least two timestamped samples, because interpolating between them is the entire mechanism. “Drop outdated ones instead of queueing them” does not merely discourage interpolation. It forbids it.

So the decision is already made, implicitly, in a line written about bandwidth rather than about smoothing. Worth making explicit, because right now the file says “let the receiving engine smooth between them” without saying how, and the how is already in the repository.

This is the third time the same commitment has decided something. The watch channel coalesces, so deltas cannot work (0002). A reconnecting client replays nothing, because absolute state supersedes it (0004). Presence drops outdated updates, so interpolation cannot work (0006). One architectural choice — latest state wins, history is not retained in transit — and every technique that needs a sequence collides with it. That sentence is the spine of this whole system, and it is a better answer to “tell me about your architecture” than any feature list.

05What you give up, stated honestly

One thing, and it is exactly the property Gambetta highlights: fidelity to the path that was actually sent. A spring is a filter. It rounds corners, lags by an amount that varies with speed, and keeps approaching after the updates stop. The motion on screen is plausible rather than real.

For an umpire's cursor and a piece being dragged, nobody will ever audit the path. The questions that matter are: does it look continuous, does it end up in the right place, and is it current enough that two umpires do not grab the same piece unawares. A spring answers all three, with no added delay and no clock.

Where it would stop being adequate — worth knowing the boundary rather than discovering it:

  1. Animating a recorded turn. If you ever play turns back as motion rather than as stills, the path is the content. That wants interpolation, and turns already carry recordedAt.
  2. Anything resolved against a moving position. You have none today. The Lua scripting goal could introduce one.
  3. Replays or spectating. Same reason: a filter cannot reconstruct what it filtered.

06The arithmetic to do before you trust it

A spring tuned for one update rate is not automatically right for another, and FOLLOW was tuned for boards, which arrive at human pace.

client/src/engine/pawns/PawnLayer.ts:39

const FOLLOW: SpringTune = { frequency: 9, damping: 0.85 };

The envelope decays as e^(−ζωt), so with ω = 2π·9 ≈ 56.5 and ζ = 0.85 the time constant is about 21 ms, and the spring is roughly 95% of the way there after about 63 ms. Presence at 20 Hz means a new target every 50 ms.

An estimate from the constants, not a measurement — this file's own convention is to say which. Treat it as a prediction to check, not a result.

Which puts you, just barely, in the good regime: the next target lands at 50 ms while the spring is still travelling, so the motion never arrives and waits. That is what continuous movement requires. But the margin is thin, and it gives you a falsifiable prediction worth testing: drop presence to 10 Hz and the spring should reach its target around 63 ms and then sit still for roughly 37 ms, which reads as stop-start.

The conclusion that transfers: the filter's settle time and the update interval are one decision, not two. If presence ends up throttled harder than 20 Hz to save bandwidth, the spring must soften to match, or you will have bought smoothness and spent it.

07So what is actually missing

Almost none of the smoothing. Two other things, and naming them is the practical point of this lesson.

  1. lifted only knows about you. The engine passes this.manipulation?.lifted() — the pieces this client is dragging. Presence needs the same visual from a different source: lifted because someone else is holding it. The spring, the tilt and the lean all work already; what is missing is a second input to that set.
  2. A cursor is not a unit. PawnLayer keys one view per unit id and deletes views for ids that vanish from the board. Presence cursors are not on the board and never will be, so they need a layer of their own — keyed by umpire, not by unit, which lands you back on identity.

Hence the ordering, which is the same one your suggested-order-of-work already implies: identity, then presence, and smoothing comes along for free. Presence cannot attribute a cursor to anyone until an umpire is more than a connection number, and “show which pieces another umpire is holding” is a sentence about identity wearing a rendering costume.

08Recall

  1. What does entity interpolation put on screen?

    • The latest position, smoothed out
    • A prediction of where entities go
    • Real server data, shown late
    • The average of recent samples

    Only data the server actually sent, delayed. It guesses nothing — that is the whole point, and the reason it needs a buffer.

  2. What does a spring in PawnLayer chase?

    • A buffer of timestamped samples
    • One target, overwritten each sync
    • The velocity of the last update
    • Two positions and a timestamp

    One target. view.motion.target = unit discards the previous one, which is precisely why no queue is needed and precisely why the exact path is unrecoverable.

  3. Which presence rule rules out interpolation?

    • Presence is never part of Doc
    • Throttle updates to twenty a second
    • Never forward presence to players
    • Drop outdated updates, never queue

    All four are real rules; only the last one is structural here. Interpolation is a queue of timestamped samples, so forbidding the queue forbids the technique.

  4. What does the spring give up?

    • Exact fidelity to the sent path
    • Smoothness between discrete updates
    • Independence from the frame rate
    • Correctness of the final position

    The path only. It still converges on the right place, and the fixed 240 Hz step keeps it frame-rate independent. A filter cannot reconstruct what it filtered.

  5. Why is lifted not ready for presence?

    • It is keyed by umpire already
    • Springs cannot express a lift
    • It reads only local manipulation
    • Players would see held pieces

    It comes from this.manipulation, so it only ever describes what this client is dragging. The rendering is done; the second input is not.

09Drill: buffer, or spring?

Pick the strategy and the reason. Several wrong options give the right strategy.

  1. Another umpire's cursor, arriving at 20 Hz.

    • Buffer: the path must match
    • Buffer: timestamps already exist
    • Spring: it arrives already smooth
    • Spring: nobody audits the path

    Spring, because path fidelity buys nothing here. Note the trap in option three: it does not arrive smooth — it arrives twenty times a second, which is exactly the problem being solved.

  2. Playing a recorded turn back as motion.

    • Spring: the tuning already exists
    • Spring: boards arrive human-paced
    • Buffer: exact path would matter
    • Buffer: turns carry no timestamps

    Buffer, because here the path is the content rather than a cosmetic. Option four is simply false — every turn carries recordedAt, which is what would make this feasible.

  3. A board arriving after another umpire's edit.

    • Buffer: two boards must blend
    • Spring: one latest target suffices
    • Buffer: coalescing loses the path
    • Spring: edits are always absolute

    Spring, and this is what ships today. Option three states something true — coalescing does lose intermediate boards — but draws the wrong conclusion: that is a reason a buffer cannot work, not a reason to want one.

10Read this next

Gabriel Gambetta, Fast-Paced Multiplayer Part III: Entity Interpolation. Short, and the clearest statement of the trade anywhere. Read it against one question: which of its assumptions do you actually have? It is written for a shooter, where positions are contested and resolved against, and almost every argument in it rests on that. Noticing which arguments survive the move to a turn-based planning tool is the skill, more than the technique itself.

Part I and Part II are worth rereading now too, because lesson 0003 drew the line between what they describe and what you built — client-side prediction with reconciliation, against your optimistic update. The three parts together are the canonical sequence, and you have now deliberately declined two of the three.

That is not a gap. Declining a technique for a stated reason is a stronger position than adopting it by default, and it is a much better interview answer.

Lesson 7 — the shape of presence. Everything above says what presence must respect; nothing yet says what it looks like on the wire. That is the first design in this series you would be writing rather than reading, and it is a good one: it must not be an Edit, must not touch Doc, must never reach a player, must coalesce per sender, and must survive its sender vanishing mid-drag. The last of those is the interesting one — there is no close event for a dropped piece.

To say out loud before then: "latest state wins, so any technique that needs the sequence — deltas, replay, interpolation — is already ruled out".
