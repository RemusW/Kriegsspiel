Lesson 0007 · Kriegsspiel network model

The shape of presence

Six lessons of reading. This one is design: nothing in the repository implements presence, and the five rules you wrote for it constrain the answer far more tightly than they look. Follow them properly and most of the work turns out to be already done — including the part you would expect to be hardest, which is what happens when someone vanishes mid-drag.

00First, from memory

  1. Which strategy does spring.ts implement?

    • A filter chasing one target
    • Buffered interpolation of real samples
    • Dead reckoning from last velocity
    • Averaging over recent positions

    A filter. One target, overwritten on every sync, no history kept — which is why it is the only strategy compatible with dropping outdated updates.

  2. Where does per-frame state belong?

    • The store, with a memo guard
    • The engine: no DOM reads it
    • The store, for devtools support
    • The engine: three.js needs it

    The engine, and the reason is that nothing rendered depends on it. “A library happens to hold it” is a coincidence; the change rate and the absence of a DOM reader are the argument.

  3. Where is a departed umpire's state cleaned up?

    • In a periodic sweep task
    • When the next umpire connects
    • Where the connection loop exits
    • In Doc::edit, on the next edit

    In client(), after serve_client returns — the single point every exit from the loop passes through. Remember that this place exists; it is about to matter again.

01The brief you already wrote

Five rules, from the presence entry in CLAUDE.md. Read as a specification rather than as notes, they rule out most of the obvious designs:

  1. Not part of Doc. Not saved, not undoable, not an Edit.
  2. Umpires only. A piece mid-drag shows where it is going, so it is hidden information.
  3. Throttled to roughly 20 a second, smoothed by the receiver.
  4. Outdated updates dropped, never queued.
  5. Show which pieces another umpire is holding, so two umpires do not grab the same piece unawares.

What is genuinely undecided: the channel it travels on, the payload, and what happens when a sender disappears. Those three, in that order, because each constrains the next.

02The decision that would quietly wreck it

The tempting move is to reuse the machinery that already works. There is a channel that wakes every connection when something changes, and presence is a change, so: send presence through changed.

Do the arithmetic before you do anything else. changed carries () — it is a doorbell, and what each connection does when it rings is call send_view, which serialises an entire board. So a drag at 20 Hz with three umpires connected is sixty whole-board snapshots a second, each one cloned and serialised separately, to communicate a cursor.

That is not a performance mistake, it is a category mistake, and it is the exact cost the delta discussion in lesson 0002 was worried about — arriving through the back door, in a design that never mentions deltas. Presence needs its own channel, and unlike the doorbell it must carry data. Reusing the doorbell for a 20 Hz stream converts a human-paced snapshot system into a real-time one by accident.

03The channel: one watch, carrying everyone

Rule four decides this, and the choice is narrower than it looks. The two candidates behave differently in a way worth getting exactly right.

broadcast is a ring buffer with a cursor per receiver. The tokio docs are explicit that it does not block the sender: “if a value is sent when the channel is at capacity, the oldest value currently held by the channel is overwritten,” and the slow receiver gets RecvError::Lagged carrying the count it missed. So it does drop — but note what the receiver then gets: its cursor moves to the oldest retained value and it carries on from there. A receiver that falls behind replays a backlog of stale cursor positions before reaching the present.

watch holds one value and nothing else. A receiver that looks away and comes back sees the current state, with no backlog to walk through, because there is no backlog.

That is the distinction rule four is reaching for. It is not “broadcast queues and watch does not” — both drop. It is that broadcast preserves the sequence and watch preserves only the latest. “Drop outdated ones instead of queueing them” is a request for the second.

Worth being precise here because the sloppy version of this argument is wrong, and you would be stating it in an interview. Broadcast is not badly behaved; it is built to deliver a stream faithfully, which is not what you want.

So: a single watch carrying the whole presence map.

// On Game, beside doc, assets and changed.
presence: watch::Sender<HashMap<UmpireId, Presence>>,

One channel, one subscription per connection, latest-wins over the entire picture. The shape is the one you already understand from changed, with a payload bolted on. Two notes on using it:

  1. An update must mutate one entry and notify, so reach for send_modify rather than read-clone-replace — the latter races two umpires moving at once. Check its exact signature; I have not read it.
  2. A receiver reads through a borrow guard, which is a lock. Never hold it across an await on a socket write — the same discipline as every other lock in this server, and the same mistake it would be.
Why whole-state is cheap here and expensive for boards

You rejected deltas and kept whole-board snapshots, then here you are sending the whole presence map on every tick. Same principle, opposite cost, and the asymmetry is the point: a presence map is a handful of umpires each holding a cursor and a few placements. A board is every unit and every map sheet. Whole-state messaging is a question of payload size, not of taste — which is why “snapshots are fine” and “serialise once per distinct view” can both be true.

04The payload: presence is an edit you have not sent

Here is the observation that makes the rest of this easy. Rule five says to show which pieces another umpire is holding, and your scenario notes want “pieces moving while another umpire drags them.” So presence must carry where the dragged pieces currently are.

You already have a type for that. Placement, with its doc comment: “Every value is absolute, so a command states the result it wants instead of how far a pointer or key moved it.” Which is exactly as true of a drag in progress as of the drag's final command.

// Client → server, at most ~20 a second.
Presence {
    cursor: Option<[f32; 2]>,
    holding: Vec<Placement>,
}

So a drag is a stream of would-be place commands, of which the last one is actually sent. Presence is the unsent prefix of an edit. Not an analogy — literally the same data in the same type, the difference being that one of them was committed.

Which also explains why presence can never be an Edit: an Edit is a before-and-after pair recorded for undo. These are proposals. The distinction your rules draw is the distinction between a value and a decision.

And the consequences cascade pleasantly. previewOf already turns Placement[] into a map of previews. PendingEdits already renders edits that are not in any board. PawnLayer already springs smoothly towards whatever targets it is handed. So the receiving side is:

  1. Feed another umpire's holding placements in as previews, alongside your own pending edits.
  2. Hand the result to PawnLayer, which smooths between the 20 Hz updates for free — lesson 0006's whole conclusion, collected here.
  3. Mark those ids as lifted, so they visibly hang from someone's pointer.

PendingEdits shows edits you have asked for and not seen confirmed. Presence shows edits someone else has not yet asked for at all. Same machinery, one more source — which is why step three of that list is the twenty-line change worth building first.

05The sender that vanishes mid-drag

The problem that sounds worst: an umpire's laptop lid closes while they are holding three pieces. There is no close event for a held piece. Does everyone else see those pieces hanging in the air for ever?

No, and you already built the fix in this session without knowing it. The cleanup point in client() runs on every exit from the loop — close frame, socket error, or the stream simply ending. One more line beside the one that is already there:

serve_client(&mut socket, &game, umpire).await;
game.forget_umpire(umpire).await;      // undo history
game.forget_presence(umpire).await;    // cursor and held pieces

That is the normal case closed completely, and it is closed structurally: there is no code path where a connection ends and this does not run. Having one exit point rather than five is what makes that statement provable instead of hopeful.

The case it does not close is a half-open connection — the peer gone without a FIN, where the socket stays notionally alive until a keepalive or TCP timeout decides otherwise, which can be minutes. client() has not returned, so nothing has been forgotten, and the ghost cursor sits there.

This is the honest limit of structural cleanup, and it is not specific to presence — it is why real systems have heartbeats. Worth knowing that your forget line is correct and still insufficient on its own.

The cheap second layer: stamp each entry with when it last arrived, and have receivers ignore entries older than a couple of seconds. That is the same self-healing posture as everything else here — nothing has to notice the failure, the display simply stops being wrong. Note the ordering: structural cleanup is the mechanism, staleness is the fallback. Doing it the other way round — relying on staleness alone — would leave a two-second ghost on every ordinary disconnect.

06One message, two destinations

On the client, lesson 0005's rule applies to the arriving message itself, and splits it:

Cursors and placementschange 20 times a second, drawn on the canvas, no DOM node reads them. Engine, beside PendingEdits.
Who is connectedchanges when someone joins or leaves, and you will want it in the UI as a list of names. Store.

Worth noticing that this is the first message in the protocol whose parts belong in different places. The split is not an awkwardness to design around — it falls straight out of asking the change-rate question of each field separately, which is what the rule is for.

07The gate that does not exist yet

Rule two says presence never reaches a player. Today that is satisfied for an uncomfortable reason: there are no players. Every connection is an umpire, so there is nobody to leak to.

Which makes this an ordering hazard rather than a bug. The moment a player role exists, presence becomes hidden information with no gate in front of it, and hidden information leaking is the one failure this project treats as unacceptable — your architecture rules already say to filter on the server and never to send a full board for the client to hide. Presence must go through the same single filtering function, for the same reason, and it must get there before players do, not after.

08Recall

  1. Why must presence not use the changed channel?

    • It carries no data payload
    • It coalesces and would drop updates
    • It wakes players as well
    • It would send whole snapshots

    Because ringing that doorbell makes every connection serialise a whole board. Option one is true but fixable; option two describes behaviour you actually want.

  2. What does watch give that broadcast does not?

    • Only ever the current value
    • Delivery without any cloning cost
    • A separate queue per receiver
    • Notification of dropped messages

    Latest-only. Both drop under load; the difference is that a lagging broadcast receiver resumes from the oldest retained value and walks a backlog of stale positions.

  3. What type should holding carry?

    • A list of unit ids
    • Positions keyed by unit id
    • The same Placement as place
    • A delta since the last frame

    Placement, unchanged. A drag in progress is a stream of would-be commands, so it carries the command's own type — absolute, idempotent, already generated by previewOf.

  4. What clears presence when a sender vanishes?

    • A close frame from the client
    • The connection loop's exit path
    • The next snapshot that is sent
    • A timeout on the watch channel

    The exit path, which runs however the loop ends — no close frame required. A staleness timeout is the second layer, for half-open connections only.

  5. Why is presence safe from players today?

    • The server filters it already
    • Effect::Everyone excludes player roles
    • No player role exists yet
    • Doc::shown removes it for them

    Because there are no players. That is an ordering hazard, not a defence: the gate has to exist before the role does.

09Drill: design judgment

No lookups here — each of these follows from a rule you wrote.

  1. An umpire's cursor stops moving for ten seconds.

    • Nothing: drop the stale entry
    • The cursor fading out slowly
    • The last position it reported
    • Its position, extrapolated forward

    Keep showing it. A resting cursor is real information — someone is there, thinking. This is why the staleness window is seconds rather than milliseconds: it must distinguish “not moving” from “gone”, and only the second is a failure.

  2. Two umpires grab the same piece at once.

    • Refuse the second umpire's drag
    • Allow both; undo sorts it out
    • Lock the piece on the server
    • Show a warning and continue

    Allow both — your own note says “if they do, per-umpire undo already copes with the result.” A server-side lock would need a new authority concept and a release-on-disconnect path, which is the kind of state you have spent this whole session avoiding. Presence makes the collision visible; it does not have to prevent it.

  3. Presence arrives while you are viewing a past turn.

    • Apply it: presence is not Doc
    • Queue it until returning to live
    • Refuse it with an error reply
    • Ignore it: the view is read-only

    Ignore it while the past is on screen. Those cursors are pointing at the live board, so drawing them over a recorded turn puts them in a world that does not exist. Queueing breaks rule four, and refusing makes no sense — presence has no replies, by design.

10Read this next

Tokio — sync::broadcast, specifically the lagging section. Short, and the only way to hold the watch-versus-broadcast argument honestly: read what Lagged actually does before claiming either channel “queues”. The sentence that matters is that returning Lagged “does not close or disconnect the receiver” — it carries on from the oldest value it still holds, which is the behaviour your rule four is quietly rejecting.

Then reread the presence entry in your own CLAUDE.md, which is the real primary source for this lesson. It is five bullets written before any of this existed, and it determined almost every answer above. Worth noticing how much work a well-written constraint does later.

If you build this, that entry should move out of open questions and into Decisions with an ADR, and the smoothing choice it leaves implicit ought to be named.

11Next

The series has caught up with the code. Seven lessons have covered why a socket, the fan-out, the client's lie, identity, where state lives, smoothing, and now presence — which is the last thing with enough design already written down to teach from. Three honest directions from here, and this one is yours to pick:

Build it — and this is the one you took: 0008 hands this design back as an exercise with tests, scoped to held pieces rather than cursors. One correction it makes to this lesson: the “twenty-line version” was only the drawing half, and the wire carries the union of what the others hold rather than a map keyed by umpire, which keeps UmpireId out of the protocol.

Go deeper on one gap. Why PendingEdits structurally cannot cover spawns and deletions is the best remaining hole in the optimistic-update story, and it is a lesson rather than a paragraph.

Leave the repo. The transferable half: backpressure, heartbeats and what actually happens to a WebSocket over a long-haul link through proxies — which RESOURCES.md still lists as a gap, and which the worldwide-play assumption in CLAUDE.md rests on entirely.

Ask me. On this lesson specifically: whether presence should be one message or two (roster and cursors have different rates, and section 06 splits them on arrival rather than on the wire — there is an argument for splitting earlier). Whether the server should validate presence at all, given that it validates every command but presence changes nothing. And whether a remotely-held piece should be sprung or snapped, which 0006 left open and which this design makes answerable.

Two things in here are unverified and marked as such: send_modify's signature, and the claim that one borrow guard is the right receiver pattern. Check both against the docs before building.
